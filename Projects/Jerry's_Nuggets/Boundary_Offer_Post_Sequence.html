Boundary Offer — Seven-Post Sequence

Spine one, elaborated. The launch text post plus six weekly videos, with the four leftover scenarios running as mid-week text posts between them. Show-then-name throughout; each video ends by trailing the next; grace toward aware leaders, never accusation.

The through-line

The whole series teaches one association: you are the guide to the boundary between human and machine thinking. Not a fixer who guarantees outcomes — an explorer-guide who's lived on the far side of a line that organizations are now crossing mostly by accident, and who can show them what it looks like from over there. Every episode demonstrates one facet of that, and the credential (thirty years, 625,000+ ideas, the world's largest published Brain) always arrives as proof-of-work rather than as the headline.

The keeper line anchors everything:

Your org is deciding right now, mostly by accident, how much of its thinking to outsource. I've spent thirty years on the other side of that line, and I can show you what it looks like from there.

The audience is the innovation executive who is already leaning into AI, positively and on purpose, and wants to explore this boundary with intention rather than fear. You're never scolding them for using AI — you're handing them the naming and the map for something they already sense.

The tone rule, in three words: invitation over admonition. Nobody in this series did anything wrong. Convenience adopted for good reasons is a frontier, not a scandal.

The arc at a glance

  1. Launch text post — the keeper line and the grace note. Copy is done; it's below.
  2. Video 1 — The four scenarios. What's quietly being decided, delivered as pattern-recognition. No footage needed; the most shippable video.
  3. Video 2 — The Brain tour. A live dispatch from the far side of the line.
  4. Video 3 — Extended mind / the good Cyborg. The reframe that turns "will it replace me?" into "what am I extending my mind with, and how do I stay in the driver's seat?"
  5. Video 4 — The GFC provenance flyover. The demonstration: signed human memory vs. a smooth, unsigned summary.
  6. Video 5 — Snip! and curation as decision. The idea underneath — every link is a choice; curated is signed, generated is unsigned.
  7. Video 6 — Design from Trust, the horizon, and the invitation. How to adopt AI so it compounds your people; a room of good Cyborgs as collective human intelligence; then the working-session invitation, planted not pitched.

Four mid-week scenario posts run in the gaps. Details at the end.

1. Launch text post — the keeper line, with the grace note: On Handing Over Your Thinking

This is the front door. It carries the whole stance in one read, and it's the post that will drive more profile visits than anything you've published in years — which is why the profile rework happens before this goes up (see the separate LinkedIn makeover file).

The copy, ready to paste:

Your organization is deciding right now — mostly by accident — how much of its thinking to outsource. Not in a strategy meeting. In a thousand small moments that arrive disguised as conveniences.

This isn't negligence. Even the leaders thinking hardest about AI haven't decided how to shape these forces, because you can't shape what you haven't seen named. Naming them is what I do.

I've spent thirty years on the other side of that line. Since before Google existed, I've been building an external memory — 625,000 ideas, over 1.2 million links, every one a decision I made and can stand behind. It's the world's largest published Brain, open for anyone to explore at JerrysBrain.com. It means the boundary between human and machine intelligence isn't a topic I research; it's where I live.

Today I guide leaders across that boundary in focused working sessions: you bring your organization's actual AI tangle, we map it live, and you leave with the map, a vector, and your next three moves.

The longer story, for the curious: I spent the 1990s as a top technology analyst (editor of Esther Dyson's Release 1.0), then decades helping organizations understand trust — founding REX, the Relationship Economy eXpedition, and Open Global Mind, and developing Design from Trust. All of it turns out to have been preparation for this boundary.

If you're leaning into these questions on purpose, write to me. I read everything.

Visual: the Brain's plex as the image, or the banner treatment — the boundary made visual.

Trailing hook: none needed here; the post itself is the series opener. Pin it to Featured the day it publishes.

2. Video 1 — The four scenarios: Ways We Give Away Our Thinking

The scenario list, delivered as sharp pattern-recognition rather than war stories. Nobody expects a boundary-dweller to have sat in their meetings, so the authority is observational, not anecdotal. Six beats, five to seven minutes.

Beat 1 — Cold open, standing on the line. Open in the keeper-line register. No throat-clearing: name that the decision is already being made, quietly, and that you've spent thirty years on the other side of it.

Beats 2–3 — The four scenarios. Four forces, each a convenience with a hidden cost. (These four are locked in as the set; the phrasings below are drafts to tune against your own ear — the smooth-consensus one is closest to final because it sets up Video 4.)

  • The unchecked summary. The AI-generated summary of the meeting or the document becomes the record. Nobody checks it against the source, so whatever it quietly dropped is simply gone — and the org now remembers the summary, not the thing.
  • The first draft that becomes final. The AI first draft is good enough to ship, so it ships. But the draft was supposed to be where thinking starts. The revision that would have surfaced the real disagreement never happens.
  • The sawn-off ladder. Juniors used to earn competence on the bottom rungs — the research memo, the rough model, the first pass. AI now does those rungs. The output is faster today; the senior people you needed ten years from now never got to climb.
  • The smooth consensus. AI answers arrive pre-averaged. The productive friction of two people who genuinely disagree — the thing that used to surface the real issue — gets replaced by text that reads plausible from every angle. (This one plants the seed Video 4's provenance demo will grow.)

Beat 4 — The reframe. "Notice: not one of these involves anyone doing anything wrong. Each one is a convenience, adopted for good reasons. That's what makes this hard — and what makes it a frontier rather than a scandal. The question isn't 'who screwed up.' It's: now that you can see these forces, how do you want to shape them?"

Beat 5 — The credential, as afterthought. "I've spent thirty years on the other side of this line — building an external memory of my own, 625,000 ideas, every link a decision I made and can stand behind. From over there, this boundary doesn't look scary. It looks like the most interesting place in the world to stand. Next week I'll take you across and show you around."

Beat 6 — The toss-back. "Which of these have you already spotted in your own organization? And which ones might be running that you haven't looked for yet? Tell me in the comments. I read them all."

Visual / thumbnail: you to camera — no Brain footage required, which is exactly why this one records first.

Trailing hook: Beat 5's last line is the trailer for Video 2. Keep it.

3. Video 2 — The Brain tour: Thinking in Space

The live dispatch. You promised to take them across; now you do. This is the episode that turns the abstract boundary into something they can watch you stand on.

The promise: show what it actually changes to have thirty years of thinking externalized, connected, and accountable — every idea linked, every link a decision. This is Brain-as-thinking-instrument, not Brain-as-storage (the Tversky territory: reasoning you do by moving through space).

The spine: open at your home node. Navigate one serious topic for about ninety seconds, then one genuinely surprising one for another ninety — the familiar/unexpected pairing that shows range without sprawl. Narrate the choices, not the software: why this link and not that one, what a branch remembers that you'd forgotten. Resist the urge to branch into every adjacent thread; one clean path beats a tangle.

Visual: screen recording of the plex in motion, your voice over it. This is the first episode that needs footage — which is another reason to record it early.

Trailing hook: close by pointing forward — you've shown them what an externalized mind does; next week, why it matters for the version of this they're living, the moment they wonder whether the machine is replacing them.

4. Video 3 — Extended mind / the good Cyborg

The pivot episode, and where the newer material earns its place. This is the reframe that dissolves the fear the whole audience is carrying.

The idea, part one — extended mind. Their cognition already doesn't stop at their skull. It never did — notebook, calendar, the colleague down the hall, the Brain on the screen. So the AI question was never "will it replace my mind?" It's "what am I extending my mind with, and who's accountable for that substrate?" That reframing takes the whole conversation out of fear and into design.

The idea, part two — the good Cyborg. The thing people dread is becoming a Cyborg. But there's a good one and a bad one, and the difference is everything. A bad Cyborg is obedient — it does what the machine suggests, defers to the smooth answer, lets its own judgment atrophy. A good Cyborg is effective — it stays in the driver's seat and uses the machine to become more itself, not less. Effective, not obedient. That's the whole distinction, and it's a choice a leader can actually make on purpose.

The community-naming move (keep this — it's the part Spine two would have thrown away). End the toss-back by genuinely asking: does "good Cyborg" land for you? Is there a better word? You're not handing down a coinage; you're inviting them to help name the thing. That's the invitation that turns readers into participants — and it's the honest version of testing whether the term has legs before you build on it.

Visual: you to camera, possibly with a simple two-column contrast on screen — obedient vs. effective — nothing fancy.

Trailing hook: "Next week I'll show you what a good Cyborg's memory looks like when the stakes are real — a trillion-dollar mistake, mapped honestly." (→ the GFC flyover.)

Optional schwag tie-in, for later: the "Be a good Cyborg!" merch idea lives naturally off this episode if it catches.

5. Video 4 — The GFC provenance flyover

The demonstration episode. Everything before this argued; this one shows.

The promise: watch provenance work when it matters. Take a single thread through the Brain — the legislation that enabled NINJA loans, the loans themselves, the 2008 consequences — and show the chain of decisions intact, each link signed by a human who made it. A ninety-second flyover on one thread, not a tour of the whole crisis.

The contrast that lands it: put that beside what a chatbot gives you when you ask about the same crisis — a smooth, pre-averaged summary that reads plausibly and carries no signature at all. Same subject; one map is accountable and one is not. That difference is the entire value proposition, made visible in two minutes.

Visual: the GFC thread in the plex, then (optionally) a screen of generic AI summary text beside it for contrast.

Trailing hook: "That accountability isn't a feature of the software. It's a decision I made 625,000 times. Next week: why every one of those links is a decision — and what gets severed when the reasoning disappears." (→ Snip!.)

6. Video 5 — Snip! and curation as decision

The idea underneath the whole practice, named.

The promise: explain Snip! — what happens when conclusions get severed from the reasoning and the consequences that produced them. The unchecked summary, the smooth consensus, the sawn-off ladder from Video 1 were all instances of one thing: decisions cut loose from their provenance, at machine speed.

The turn: and here's the antidote you've been living. Every link in the Brain is a decision. The difference between curated knowledge and generated knowledge is the difference between signed and unsigned. The Brain's value was never the volume — it's that each of those 1.2 million links is a choice someone can stand behind. Curation is decision-making, made durable.

Visual: a link being drawn in the plex — the small, deliberate act — contrasted with a wall of auto-generated text.

Trailing hook: "So if that's the problem — and if signed memory is the antidote — the real question is how a whole organization adopts AI without severing itself from its own thinking. That's next week, and it's the one I care about most." (→ Design from Trust.)

7. Video 6 — Design from Trust, the horizon, and the invitation

The closer. Three moves: the stance, the horizon, the door.

The stance — Design from Trust. How do you adopt AI so it compounds your people rather than polices or replaces them? You design from trust rather than from suspicion. Most institutions armor themselves against their own members cheating, and end up too armored to think. Applied to AI, Design from Trust means building adoption that assumes and rewards good judgment — that makes people more capable, more themselves, more effective Cyborgs.

The horizon — collective human intelligence. Then lift the frame one level, gently. Picture a room full of good Cyborgs, each carrying provenanced, accountable memory, sharing it with each other. That's not a team using a tool. That's collective human intelligence — and it's the thing actually worth building. Name Big Fungus lightly here, as the invitation-to-come rather than a pitch: the idea that our thinking tools could find each other and leave the shared territory better than they found it.

The door — the working session. Now, and only now, the offer, planted not harvested: if any of this is live for you, there's a way to work on it directly. You bring your organization's actual AI tangle; we map it live in a focused session; you leave with the map, a vector, and your next three moves. No transformation promised — just a clearer frontier and a real next step. "If you're leaning into these questions on purpose, write to me. I read everything."

Visual: close on the plex, or on the boundary banner — one image that says the whole series in a glance.

Trailing hook: this one trails the session, not another video. The comment prompt invites the inquiry.

The four mid-week scenario posts

These fill the gaps between videos and keep the theme warm. Each is a single-scenario text riff in the same grace-toned, pattern-recognition voice — no video, low effort, high continuity. Post one between each pair of videos.

The disappearing question. People stop asking colleagues things, because the machine answers faster. Every unasked question is a thread not woven — the informal fabric of who-knows-what quietly thins. (Your orality / social-fabric theme wearing a business suit.) Close: what did your team used to ask each other that they now ask the machine?

The provenance vacuum. A decision gets made on a number or a claim, and when someone finally asks "where did this come from?", the honest answer is: nobody knows. Nothing in the chain has a signature. Close: could your organization reconstruct the source of the last big number it acted on?

The prompt-shaped problem. Teams start framing their problems only in ways the tool can answer. The questions narrow to fit the instrument, and nobody notices the questions that stopped being askable. Close: what's a question worth asking that no prompt would ever surface?

The unauditable decision. Something goes wrong, and no one can reconstruct the reasoning that led there — the chain of thought lived in a context window that's gone. Decisions severed from their consequences, at machine speed. Close: when your last decision goes sideways, will the reasoning still exist to learn from?
