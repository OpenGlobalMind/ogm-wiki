Ways We Give Away Our Thinking

(draft) Subhed.

Beat 1 — Cold open, standing on the line. Open in the keeper-line register. No throat-clearing: name that the decision is already being made, quietly, and that you've spent thirty years on the other side of it.

Last post I flagged that Your org is deciding right now, mostly by accident, how much of its thinking to outsource. I've spent thirty years on the other side of that line, and I can show you what it looks like from there.

First, let me describe the natural creep with four examples.

Beats 2–5 — The four scenarios. Four forces, each a convenience with a hidden cost. (These four are locked in as the set; the phrasings below are drafts to tune against your own ear — the smooth-consensus one is closest to final because it sets up Video 4.)

  • Unchecked Summaries. Are you using Otter, Fireflies, Fathom, Granola or Zoom's built-in AI meeting summaries? The AI-generated summary of the meeting or the document becomes the record. Nobody checks it against the source, so whatever it quietly dropped is simply gone — and the org now remembers the summary, not the thing. Ever heard the advice that whoever takes notes of a meeting has way more power than most people realize? That's what's happening here.
  • The AI First Draft that Becomes Final. The AI first draft is good enough to ship, so it ships. But the draft was supposed to be where thinking starts. The revision that would have surfaced the real disagreement never happens.
  • The Sawn-Off Ladder. Juniors used to earn competence on the bottom rungs — the research memo, the rough model, the first pass. AI now does those rungs. The output is faster today; the senior people you needed ten years from now never got to climb. Story: New Science Stuffing Parties,
  • Nobody's Consensus. AI answers arrive pre-averaged. The productive friction of two people who genuinely disagree — the thing that used to surface the real issue — gets replaced by text that reads plausible from every angle. (This one plants the seed Video 4's provenance demo will grow.)

Beat 6 — Cognitive Offloading vs Cognitive Surrender. As Beth Kanter pointed out in a comment to my previous post, the theory behind these dynamics contrasts cognitive offloading with cognitive surrender.

Offloading is the positive goal: hand the tasks that AI is great at to the AIs, while staying in the middle of your work.

Surrender, as it sounds, is not the desirable outcome. It's the road to us humans putting ourselves out to pasture. It's also easy to slip onto, because the list of things that AIs are great at keeps growing.

The difference between offloading and surrender is sometimes hard to see early on. It's not like surrender scenarios have you threatening someone with a gun, Pulp Fiction style. It's subtle.

Beat 7 — The reframe. "Notice: not one of these involves anyone doing anything wrong. Each one is a convenience, adopted for good reasons.

That's what makes this hard — and what makes it a frontier rather than a scandal. The question isn't 'who screwed up.' It's: now that you can see these forces, how do you want to shape them?"

Beat 8 — The credential, as afterthought. "I've spent thirty years on the Offloading side of this line — building an external memory of my own, 645,000 ideas, every link a decision I made and can stand behind.

From over there, this boundary doesn't look scary. It looks like the most interesting place in the world to stand. Next post I'll take you across and show you around."

Beat 9 — The toss-back. "Which of these have you already spotted in your own organization? And which ones might be running that you haven't looked for yet? Tell me in the comments. I read them all."

Visual / thumbnail: you to camera — no Brain footage required, which is exactly why this one records first.
